# Calculating with Formulas Intro Video Pack

Mission: Lesson 23 - Calculating with Formulas

## Learning Outcome

By the end of this intro video the learner can use the difference of two squares formula, a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b), as a calculation shortcut. They can subtract two squares quickly, rewrite hidden squares (a product like 17 × 17, or a plain number like 4, 49 or 1) so the formula applies, multiply two numbers either side of a round number, collapse a long alternating chain of consecutive squares into a simple running total, factor spaced square pairs whose bases differ by more than one, and spot that the two neighbours of a number multiply to one less than its square.

## Named Skills Shown Before Practice

- Difference of two squares
- Squares in disguise
- Products near a round number
- Consecutive square chain
- Spaced square pairs
- Spot the neighbours

Each named skill below appears as one storyboard scene, one intro-scene title in the module JS, and one entry in the registry skills array, so the vocabulary stays identical everywhere the child meets it.

## Storyboard

1. **Difference of two squares**
   Purpose: meet the formula a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b) and use it to subtract two squares.
   Voiceover: Start with the big idea. Take a square of side a and cut out a square of side b from one corner, then rearrange the left-over L-shape into a rectangle. It measures a plus b along one side and a minus b along the other, so a squared minus b squared equals a plus b times a minus b.

2. **Squares in disguise**
   Purpose: rewrite products and plain numbers as squares so the formula applies.
   Voiceover: Sometimes the squares are hiding. A product such as seventeen times seventeen is just seventeen squared, and a plain number like forty nine, four or one is really seven squared, two squared or one squared. Once you rewrite both parts as squares, the difference of squares formula clicks straight in.

3. **Products near a round number**
   Purpose: multiply two numbers either side of a round number with (n − d)(n + d).
   Voiceover: Here is a multiplication shortcut. Nineteen times twenty one has nineteen sitting one below twenty and twenty one one above twenty, so it equals twenty squared minus one squared. Whenever two numbers are the same distance either side of a round number, multiply them as that round number squared minus the distance squared.

4. **Consecutive square chain**
   Purpose: pair a long alternating chain of consecutive squares into a running total.
   Voiceover: Long chains look frightening but they collapse neatly. Group the chain into pairs like a squared minus a minus one squared. Each pair factors to a plus a minus one, which is just the two bases added together, so the whole chain turns into one plus two plus three all the way up, a friendly running total you can add.

5. **Spaced square pairs**
   Purpose: factor each pair when the bases differ by more than one before adding.
   Voiceover: The chain still works when the bases are further apart. In eighty squared minus seventy eight squared and onwards, each pair has bases two apart, but a squared minus b squared always factors into a plus b times a minus b. Factor every pair into an easy product, then add all of those products together.

6. **Spot the neighbours**
   Purpose: see that n × n − (n − 1)(n + 1) equals one and similar near-square tricks.
   Voiceover: Watch for the neighbours of a square. Two thousand and fifteen times itself minus two thousand and sixteen times two thousand and fourteen looks enormous, but the second product is two thousand and fifteen squared minus one, so subtracting leaves just one. Neighbours of a number always multiply to one less than the number squared.

## Warmup Gate

Before full practice, the learner should answer:

1. Write 50² − 49² as (50 + 49) × (50 − 49). What does each bracket come to, and what is the answer?
2. Why is 17 × 17 − 49 the same as 17² − 7², and what is the difference of those two squares?
3. In 100² − 99² + 98² − 97² − … − 1², why does grouping into pairs turn the whole thing into 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 100?

## Misconception Risks

These are the common pitfalls children bring to calculating with formulas:

- Thinking a² − b² equals (a − b)² or (a − b), instead of the product (a + b)(a − b).
- Failing to recognise a hidden square, so 17 × 17 − 49 or 98² − 4 is not rewritten as a difference of two squares.
- In a chain, forgetting to pair the terms and instead trying to square and subtract every number one at a time.
- Forgetting that the two neighbours of n multiply to n² − 1, so n × n − (n − 1)(n + 1) is read as a huge number rather than just 1, like Pip in the lesson's reasoning task.

## Voiceover Draft

This is the full voiceover draft. Each paragraph maps to one storyboard scene and is written to be read aloud to an eleven or twelve year old, naming the skill and pointing at what changes on screen during that beat of the intro.

Difference of two squares. Take a square of side a and cut a square of side b out of one corner. Slide the left-over L-shape into a neat rectangle and measure it: one side is a plus b, the other is a minus b. That is why a squared minus b squared is exactly a plus b times a minus b, the formula we will use all lesson.

Squares in disguise. Now the squares hide. Seventeen times seventeen is simply seventeen squared, and a lonely number like forty nine, four or one is really seven squared, two squared or one squared. Rewrite both halves as squares, and a tricky looking sum becomes an ordinary difference of two squares ready to factor.

Products near a round number. Look for a round number sitting in the middle. Nineteen and twenty one are each one step from twenty, so nineteen times twenty one is twenty squared minus one squared, which is four hundred take away one. A hard multiplication becomes a tiny subtraction once you find that centre.

Consecutive square chain. Watch the long chain of squares with plus and minus signs. Pair them up: a squared minus a minus one squared. Each pair factors to a plus a minus one, the two bases added, so the entire chain becomes one plus two plus three and onwards, a running total that is easy to finish.

Spaced square pairs. This time the bases inside each pair are two, three or four apart. The formula still holds: a squared minus b squared is a plus b times a minus b. Factor each pair into a small friendly product, line them up, and add them to reach the total.

Spot the neighbours. Finally, beware of numbers that are neighbours of a square. Two thousand and fifteen times itself minus two thousand and sixteen times two thousand and fourteen looks gigantic, yet the second product is two thousand and fifteen squared minus one. Take it away and only one is left, because the two neighbours of a number always multiply to one less than its square.
