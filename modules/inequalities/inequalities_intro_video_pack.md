# Inequalities Intro Video Pack

Mission: Lesson 25 - Inequalities

## Learning Outcome

By the end of this intro video the learner can work with inequalities. They can read the four symbols (<, >, ≤, ≥) and know that ≤ and ≥ allow the two sides to be equal; use the properties of inequalities — adding or subtracting the same value, or multiplying or dividing by a positive number, keeps the direction of the arrow; solve a linear inequality like an equation and pick the smallest or largest integer, taking care that a strict < or > excludes the boundary value while ≤ or ≥ includes it; count how many positive integers satisfy an inequality; and turn an "at least / more than" word problem into an inequality, solve it, and round up to a whole number of items.

## Named Skills Shown Before Practice

- Recognise inequality symbols
- Inequality properties
- Smallest integer solution
- Largest integer solution
- Inequality word problems
- Count integer solutions

Each named skill below appears as one storyboard scene, one intro-scene title in the module JS, and one entry in the registry skills array, so the vocabulary stays identical everywhere the child meets it.

## Storyboard

1. **Recognise inequality symbols**
   Purpose: read the four symbols and know that ≤ and ≥ also allow equality.
   Voiceover: There are four inequality symbols to know, less than, greater than, less than or equal to, and greater than or equal to. The little line underneath the last two means equal is allowed, so a statement like seventy five is less than or equal to seventy five is actually true, because the two sides are equal.

2. **Inequality properties**
   Purpose: do the same thing to both sides and the arrow keeps its direction.
   Voiceover: Inequalities behave like balance scales, so whatever you do to one side you must do to the other. Adding or subtracting the same number keeps the arrow pointing the same way, and so does multiplying or dividing both sides by a positive number, so if a is greater than b then a plus three is still greater than b plus three.

3. **Smallest integer solution**
   Purpose: solve like an equation, then watch the boundary for a strict greater-than.
   Voiceover: To find the smallest whole number, solve the inequality exactly as if it were an equation, collecting the letters on one side and the numbers on the other. Suppose you reach x is greater than nine; because the symbol is a strict greater than, nine itself does not count, so the smallest whole number that works is ten.

4. **Largest integer solution**
   Purpose: solve, then step just inside the boundary for the largest value.
   Voiceover: Finding the largest whole number works the same way. Solve to get something like x is less than or equal to eight, where eight is allowed and is the largest; but for a strict less than twelve, twelve is not allowed, so the largest whole number that fits is eleven, one step inside the boundary.

5. **Inequality word problems**
   Purpose: turn an "at least" story into an inequality and round up.
   Voiceover: Word problems become inequalities. Zoey wants to give thirty three friends at least five lollipops each, so she needs one hundred and sixty five; she has twelve and buys packs of seven, which gives twelve plus seven p is at least one hundred and sixty five. Solving gives p at least about twenty one point nine, and since you cannot buy part of a pack you round up to twenty two.

6. **Count integer solutions**
   Purpose: solve for the range, then count the whole numbers inside it.
   Voiceover: Some questions ask how many whole numbers fit. Solve the inequality to find the range; for example fifteen x minus sixty four less than nine x plus eight becomes six x less than seventy two, so x is less than twelve. The positive whole numbers that fit are one, two, three, up to eleven, which is eleven values in total.

## Warmup Gate

Before full practice, the learner should answer:

1. Why is the statement 75 ≤ 75 true, even though the two numbers are equal?
2. If a > b, what symbol goes in the blank: a + 3 ___ b + 3, and why does adding the same number not change the direction?
3. Solving an inequality gives x > 9. Why is the smallest whole number 10 and not 9?

## Misconception Risks

These are the common pitfalls children bring to inequalities:

- Including the boundary value for a strict inequality, like Pip in the lesson who solved x > 6 and then said the smallest value is 6 instead of 7.
- Reading ≤ or ≥ as strict and forgetting that the two sides are allowed to be equal (so 8 ≥ 8 is true).
- For word problems, rounding the wrong way — rounding down when you need "at least", instead of rounding up to the next whole item.
- Forgetting that the rule "the arrow keeps its direction" only holds when you multiply or divide by a positive number (the lesson stays with positive numbers, but the habit of checking matters later).

## Voiceover Draft

This is the full voiceover draft. Each paragraph maps to one storyboard scene and is written to be read aloud to an eleven or twelve year old, naming the skill and pointing at what changes on screen during that beat of the intro.

Recognise inequality symbols. Four arrows slide onto the screen: less than, greater than, less than or equal to, and greater than or equal to. Notice the little line under the last two; it whispers "equal is allowed". That is why seventy five is less than or equal to seventy five is a true statement, because the two sides are simply equal.

Inequality properties. Picture a balance scale that is tipped to one side. Whatever you add to one pan you add to the other, and the tilt stays the same. Adding or subtracting the same number keeps the arrow pointing the same way, and so does multiplying or dividing both sides by a positive number, so a greater than b becomes a plus three greater than b plus three.

Smallest integer solution. Solve the inequality just like an equation, sliding the letters to one side and the numbers to the other, until you reach something like x greater than nine. Now look at the number line; nine sits under an open circle because the strict greater than leaves it out, so the smallest whole number that lands inside the shaded part is ten.

Largest integer solution. The largest whole number is found the same way. If you reach x less than or equal to eight, the circle on eight is filled in, so eight is allowed and is the largest. But if you reach a strict less than twelve, the circle on twelve is open, so you step back to eleven, the largest whole number that still fits.

Inequality word problems. Read the story carefully and build an inequality. Zoey needs one hundred and sixty five lollipops, already has twelve, and buys packs of seven, giving twelve plus seven p is at least one hundred and sixty five. Solve it to get p is at least about twenty one point nine, then round up, because half a pack is not for sale, landing on twenty two packs.

Count integer solutions. Sometimes the question is how many. Solve to find the range, like six x less than seventy two, which means x less than twelve. On the number line the dots march one, two, three, all the way up to eleven, stopping just before the open circle on twelve, so there are eleven positive whole numbers in total.
